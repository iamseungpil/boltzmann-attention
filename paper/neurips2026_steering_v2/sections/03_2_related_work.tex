\section{Related Work}

The closest prior line is spectral key-space steering. SEKA learns low-rank projections from synthetic question-answer pairs and injects them into attention keys during inference; AdaSEKA extends that recipe with a small expert ensemble and an input-dependent expert selector \citep{li2026seka}. That family belongs to the broader inference-time steering literature, which also includes activation-space direction methods such as ITI and CAA \citep{li2023iti,rimsky2024caa}, spectral activation editing \citep{qiu2024sea}, post-softmax attention steering \citep{zhang2024pasta}, prompt-anchoring methods \citep{tian2025spa}, and key/query focus-direction methods for long-context relevance control \citep{zhu2025focusdirections}. The common theme is intervention without finetuning, but the intended effect is usually to preserve, amplify, or recover a chosen signal.

Our paper differs first in the \emph{task}. We study multi-tool selection, where the model must emit two tools in sequence on the same prompt. This changes the failure mode. A method that keeps amplifying the same facet can look reasonable on a single-selection benchmark and still be mismatched for sequential coverage, because the second decoding step should become less like the first one, not more. The relevant comparison to SEKA and AdaSEKA is therefore not ``which low-rank edit is bigger,'' but ``which intervention matches the structure of sequential coverage.''

The paper also differs in the \emph{intervention site and direction source}. The evaluated positive result comes from a query-side operator, not from key amplification, and the basis is derived from the tool catalog rather than learned from synthetic contrastive pairs. This distinction matters. AdaSEKA makes the selected key-side projector input-dependent, but the operation is still to amplify the chosen subspace. ITI and CAA edit activations along learned behavior directions, but they are not formulated as multi-tool coverage operators. Focus Directions changes key/query behavior to increase attention to relevant context, but its objective is long-context relevance rather than sequential tool diversity. Our method changes the sign semantics themselves: a negative query-space coefficient suppresses already dominant ontology-aligned components and therefore acts like a coarse coverage prior rather than a highlighting mechanism.

More broadly, the paper belongs to post-hoc activation steering rather than training-time routing. The intervention is applied at inference time through attention-space hooks, with no additional finetuning and no external selector network. That choice is deliberate. It lets the experiment isolate a narrow claim that can be cleanly tested against SEKA-style methods: before adding explicit history tracking or learned routing, does the combination of ontology geometry, intervention site, and sign already determine whether the effect encourages coverage or repetition?

KV-cache compression is orthogonal to the main question here. Methods such as KIVI study low-bit cache representations and memory-quality tradeoffs rather than tool-selection dynamics \citep{liu2024kivi}. The evaluated models are instruction-tuned Qwen, Llama, and Mistral variants \citep{qwen2024qwen25,grattafiori2024llama3,jiang2023mistral}, and the main benchmark is MetaTool Subtask4 because it directly exposes the sequential-coverage problem that stationary single-facet steering is expected to mishandle.
