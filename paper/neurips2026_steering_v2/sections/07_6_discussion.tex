\section{Discussion}

The paper is strongest as a scoped mechanism paper, not as a universal steering framework. The Q-side gain is real but small. The K-side stability result is much larger and more decisive, but it is a negative lesson about where stationary key-space steering fails on multi-tool prompts. Keeping those two facts separate improves the paper's coherence.

There are three limitations. First, the evaluated Q-side operator is a history-free approximation. It is motivated by an ideal coverage mechanism, but it does not explicitly track emitted facets. Second, the multi-tool result is currently demonstrated on a single benchmark family. Third, the paper does not provide a clean, matched, full-scale comparison against an external SEKA or AdaSEKA implementation on the exact same models, prompts, and decoding setup. That missing comparison should be read as a scope boundary, not hidden inside the main claim.

These limitations still leave a useful contribution. The paper identifies a stable sign pattern, isolates basis specificity with strong null controls, and removes an easy alternative explanation based on perturbation magnitude alone. That is enough to support the narrower claim that ontology-guided query suppression is a better direction than stationary key amplification for the current multi-tool setting. It also clarifies how a future matched comparison should be designed: the correct question is not simply whether SEKA-style projections help, but whether any stationary key-side method can remain competitive once the benchmark requires sequential facet coverage rather than one-shot emphasis.
