\section{Discussion}

The empirical picture has two parts. The first is a modest but consistent positive result for query-side suppression. The second is a much larger negative result for stationary key-side amplification on the same task. Taken together, these findings suggest that multi-tool selection is governed less by generic low-rank emphasis and more by whether the intervention encourages coverage or repetition.

This interpretation is strengthened by the null controls. The ontology basis is not merely a weak perturbation that happens to do less damage. It produces the largest effective perturbation magnitude while remaining the only stable direction under the K-side comparison and the only basis that preserves the Q-side gain. The practical implication is that geometry and intervention site must be considered jointly: basis quality alone does not determine behavior, and neither does intervention strength in isolation.

\subsection{What the current evidence already rules out}

The current evidence already rules out one broad family of explanations. First, the effect is not a generic low-rank perturbation: random and feature-shuffled controls do not reproduce it. Second, it is not just a weak-edit phenomenon: the real ontology basis perturbs more than the controls and still remains the only stable useful direction. Third, it is not enough to say that an operator is low-rank and inference-time. On this task, the sign and intervention site determine whether the edit behaves like coverage control or like repeated highlighting.

These observations also explain why the closest-prior comparison must be evaluated under a locked protocol. A nominal comparison to SEKA or AdaSEKA is meaningful only when scorer, prompt formatting, decoding policy, and source basis handling are matched. Otherwise a reviewer cannot tell whether a measured gap reflects mechanism or evaluation mismatch. The present paper therefore argues first for a task-structure mismatch and only second for a benchmark ranking.

The remaining interpretive question is how directly the observed F1 shift reflects sequential coverage. The most informative analysis is a stepwise one: first-tool accuracy, second-tool accuracy, and repeated-first-facet failure. That is the cleanest bridge between the structural theory and the benchmark outcome because it tests whether the operator is changing the second decision in the direction that the sign semantics predict.
